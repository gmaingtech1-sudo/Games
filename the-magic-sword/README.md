## Chapter One: The Boy Who Wasn't Brave

Leo Smith was eleven years old, and he was afraid of almost everything.

He was afraid of the dark, especially the particular dark that lived in the cupboard under the stairs. He was afraid of deep water, and high places, and dogs that barked without warning. He was afraid of the Pritchard twins, who were in the year above him and had fists like bags of marbles. He was afraid of the moment in class when Mrs Okafor looked up from the register, smiled, and said, "Let's hear from... Leo."

Some of the children at school called him Leo the Lion. They did not mean it kindly.

But there was one thing Leo Smith was not afraid of, and that was broken things.

A broken thing, as far as Leo could tell, was just a thing waiting for somebody to be patient with it. He had mended his mum's hairdryer with a screwdriver and a library book. He had fixed the wobble in the kitchen table, the squeak in the back gate, and an old radio he'd found in a skip, which now played nothing but a crackly station of old jazz that he loved anyway. Broken things did not laugh at you. They didn't expect you to be brave. They just waited, quietly, for someone to take the time.

So when his mum told him he would be spending the summer at Grandad's, Leo was not excited. Grandad's house was a very long drive away, in a village called Hollowford, at the end of a lane, at the edge of a wood. It had no internet, one dog, and a forge — and a forge, Leo was fairly sure, was mostly fire.

"It's only for the summer," Mum said, as the car bumped down the lane between hedges so tall they met overhead like a tunnel. "The hospital needs me on nights, love. I can't leave you on your own."

"I know," said Leo.

"And you'll like it. Grandad's lovely. You liked him at Christmas."

Leo had liked him at Christmas. Grandad had come to stay for three days, and he had been so enormous that he'd had to duck under every doorway in their flat, and he'd laughed so loudly at the crackers that the neighbours had banged on the wall. He had brought Leo a present: a small iron dragon, no bigger than Leo's thumb, with wings that really folded. Leo kept it on his windowsill. He had looked at it every day since, trying to work out how anyone could make something so small and so perfect out of something as stubborn as iron.

The tunnel of hedges ended. The car rolled into a yard of cobbles and weeds, and there was the cottage — grey stone, green door, roses climbing round the windows — and beside it a squat stone building with a tall black chimney breathing a lazy ribbon of smoke into the sky.

The forge.

Something came barrelling out of the green door, barking.

Leo's heart jumped into his throat. Then the something stopped, sat down, and sneezed.

It was the scruffiest dog Leo had ever seen. It was the colour of a digestive biscuit, which Leo supposed was the point, with a beard like an old sailor and eyebrows so bushy they looked stuck on. It gave Leo a long, thoughtful look through the car window, as though it were making up its mind about him.

"That's Biscuit," said Mum. "He's about a hundred years old. He won't hurt you."

Behind Biscuit came Grandad.

Walter Smith was the biggest man Leo had ever known. He had a white beard, a leather apron scorched black at the edges, and forearms criss-crossed with little pale scars, the kind you get from sparks. His hands were the size of dinner plates. When he smiled, his whole face folded up like a map.

"There he is!" Grandad boomed. "There's my boy!"

He hugged Leo so hard Leo's feet left the ground. He smelled of coal smoke and soap and, very faintly, of sausages.

Mum stayed for a cup of tea. Then she stood by the car for a long time, fussing with Leo's collar and telling Grandad about bedtimes and vegetables.

"Don't let him live on sausages, Dad."

"Sausages!" Grandad looked wounded. "What do you take me for? There'll be chips as well."

Mum laughed, and hugged Leo, and hugged him again, and then the car was bumping away up the lane and Leo was standing in the yard with his rucksack, an enormous grandad, and a dog that was still looking at him as if it were thinking something.

"Right then," said Grandad, clapping his huge hands together. "Tea. Then the tour."

* * *

The tour did not take long. There was the cottage, which was small and crooked and full of things: clocks that ticked out of time with each other, shelves of books with cracked spines, and a fireplace with two iron firedogs on the hearth, shaped like lions curled up asleep. Leo's room was in the roof, under the slope of the ceiling, with a window that looked straight out across the yard at the forge.

Then there was the forge itself.

Inside, it was dim and warm and smelled of hot metal. In the middle stood the anvil, black and heavy and solid as a rock. Along one wall was a big stone hearth where the coals glowed orange under a hood of blackened brick, and beside it hung an enormous pair of leather bellows. Tools hung everywhere — tongs, hammers, chisels, punches — each on its own hook, each oiled and gleaming.

"Three rules in the forge," said Grandad, holding up three thick fingers. "One. Never touch anything near the fire without gloves. Iron doesn't have to glow to be hot. Two. Never leave the forge with the fire untended." He paused. "Three. The old chest stays shut."

He nodded to the back of the forge. In the shadows sat a long chest of dark oak, bound with iron bands, with a heavy lock on the front.

"What's in it?" asked Leo.

"Old things," said Grandad. "Family things." And he said it in a way that made Leo understand the conversation was over.

But it wasn't the chest Leo was looking at. It was the wall behind it.

Set into the stone at the back of the forge was a door.

It was small — barely taller than Leo himself — and arched at the top, made of black oak so old it looked almost like stone. It was studded with iron nails. It had no handle. It had only a keyhole, and the keyhole was a strange shape: not a plain slot, but a round hole with notches all around its edge, like the petals of a flower. Or a mane.

"Where does that go?" said Leo.

Grandad was quiet for a moment.

"Nowhere you need to worry about," he said at last. "Come on. Those sausages won't cook themselves."

That evening, while Grandad was washing up, Leo walked round the outside of the forge. He went round the back, past the woodpile and the rhubarb and the rows of runner beans, until he was standing exactly where the little door should come out.

There was nothing there. Just a wall — solid grey stone, covered in ivy, without so much as a crack.

Leo put his hand flat against it. The stone was cool and ordinary.

A door that went nowhere, he thought. He didn't like that at all.

* * *

He couldn't sleep that night. The cottage made noises his flat never made: creaks and ticks and the long sighing of the wind in the chimney. The dark in the roof room was a thick, country kind of dark, much darker than the orange streetlight dark he was used to. He lay under the covers with the little iron dragon in his fist and told himself that he was eleven, and eleven was far too old to be scared of the dark.

It didn't help.

At some point, very late, a sound woke him.

*Clang.*

*Clang.*

*Clang.*

Leo crept to the window. Across the yard, the forge windows glowed orange. Somebody was hammering.

He opened the window a crack, and the night air slid in, cool and smelling of honeysuckle. Between the hammer blows he could hear his grandad's voice — low and tired, not booming at all.

"...I know, old boy. I know. But he's only eleven."

And then a second voice answered. It was gruff and deep and rumbly, and Leo couldn't make out a single word. It sounded almost like a growl.

Leo stood at the window until his feet were freezing. But the hammering didn't start again, and the voices didn't come back, and after a while the forge went dark.

In the morning he told himself he had dreamed it.

He hadn't.

## Chapter Two: The Chest in the Forge

For a whole week, nothing strange happened at all — unless you counted the fact that Leo turned out to be good at something.

Grandad put him to work on the very first morning. Leo pumped the bellows until the coals roared white and yellow. He held the tongs while Grandad hammered, and learned to plunge hot iron into the water barrel, where it hissed and spat like an angry cat. He learned that iron changes colour as it heats — dull red, then cherry, then orange, then a yellow so bright you have to squint — and that each colour means it is ready for something different.

He was clumsy at first. He dropped things. He jumped every time a spark flew. But on the third day a farmer came by with a broken gate hinge, and Grandad was busy, and so — very carefully, very slowly — Leo mended it himself.

Grandad turned the hinge over in his great hands for a long time.

"Who taught you to do that?"

"Nobody," said Leo. "I just... looked at it until I could see how it wanted to go back together."

Grandad gave him a strange look — not a cross look, more like the look of someone who has just found something they lost a long time ago.

"That's the whole secret, that is," he said. "Anything broken can be mended, if you're patient with it." He handed the hinge back. "Iron's like people, Leo. Hit it too hard while it's cold and it'll crack on you. Warm it through first. Then it'll let you shape it."

That evening, Grandad showed him the bowl.

It sat on the kitchen dresser, and Leo had noticed it before: a plain white china bowl with thin lines of gold zigzagging across it like lightning.

"Your grandma dropped it the week we got married," said Grandad. "Smashed it in seven pieces. She cried her eyes out — it had been her mother's. So I stuck it back together, and I filled the cracks with gold." He ran his thumb along one of the golden lines. "She said it was more beautiful mended than it ever was whole."

Leo looked at the bowl for a long time. He thought his grandma had been right.

On the mantelpiece, above the sleeping iron lions, was a photograph Leo hadn't noticed before. It was black and white and faded, and it showed two boys standing in front of the forge. One was small and skinny, with sticking-out ears and a shy grin. The other was taller and older, with his arms folded and his chin up, as if he were daring the camera to argue with him.

"Is that you?" Leo asked, pointing at the small one.

"It is."

"Who's the other boy?"

Grandad took a while to answer.

"My brother," he said. "Edwin."

"I didn't know you had a brother."

"Not many people do any more." Grandad set the photograph down very gently, as though it might break. "He went away when we were boys. A long time ago now."

"Where did he go?"

But Grandad had already turned away, and was asking Leo whether he wanted beans or peas with his tea, in a voice that was trying very hard to be ordinary.

* * *

It was the next morning that Leo first noticed the rust.

It was on the tongs. It was on the hammers. It was on the chisels and the punches and the files — a fine orange bloom, like pollen, on every tool in the forge. Leo knew Grandad oiled every tool every night. He had watched him do it.

"Damp," said Grandad shortly, and oiled them all again.

The next morning the rust was back, thicker.

The morning after that, it was on the anvil.

And the morning after *that*, Leo came into the forge early and found words written across the black face of the anvil in rust — rough red letters, as though someone had scrawled them with a finger:

**I HAVE WAITED LONG ENOUGH.**

Leo stared at them. His skin prickled all over.

Behind him, the door creaked. Grandad came in, saw the words, and went very still. Then he took a rag from his apron pocket and wiped them away, hard, until the anvil was black again.

"Grandad," said Leo, "what was —"

"Nothing."

"It wasn't nothing. It was *writing*."

Grandad turned round. For the first time since Leo had arrived, he looked old — not big and booming, just old, and tired, and frightened.

"Leo," he said quietly, "some things are my job to worry about. This is one of them. All right?"

Leo wanted to argue. Instead, he nodded.

But he couldn't help noticing that the iron studs on the little black door were weeping thin orange trails of rust down the wood, like tears.

* * *

That night, Biscuit woke him.

The old dog was barking — not a normal bark, but a deep, frantic, over-and-over bark that Leo could feel through the floorboards. He sat bolt upright. His room was pitch black.

"Grandad?"

No answer.

Leo fumbled for his torch. His hands were shaking so badly that the beam jittered all over the walls. He crept down the narrow stairs. Grandad's bedroom door was open. The bed was empty.

Biscuit was at the back door, scratching and whining. The moment Leo opened it, the dog shot across the yard to the forge.

The forge door stood wide open. Inside, the fire was still glowing low. The little black door at the back was shut.

But it wasn't the door that made Leo's breath catch. It was the wall around it.

Rust was spreading out from the door frame, across the stones — creeping, slowly, visibly, in feathery orange patterns like frost on a window. As Leo watched, a hammer hanging nearby crumbled into red flakes and pattered to the floor.

On the anvil was a folded sheet of paper, weighed down with a horseshoe. Leo snatched it up and read it by torchlight.

> *Leo,*
>
> *I've had to go and see to something. If I'm not back by morning, don't worry. Stay in the house, look after Biscuit, and ring your mum.*
>
> *If the rust reaches the house, open the chest. The key is where the lions sleep. Take what's inside, take Biscuit, and go as far from the forge as you can.*
>
> *Do NOT open the little door. Whatever happens.*
>
> *I love you, lad. You're braver than you think.*
>
> *— Grandad*

Leo read it four times. Then he sat down on the cold floor of the forge, next to Biscuit, and waited for morning.

Grandad did not come back.

* * *

In the morning, Leo tried to ring his mum. The phone in the hall made a noise like rusty nails being shaken in a tin, and then went dead. His own mobile had no signal. It never had signal at Grandad's.

By lunchtime, the rust had crawled out of the forge and across the yard. The cobbles were orange. The weeds between them had turned to brittle, rust-red metal, and when Leo touched one, it snapped off with a tiny tinkling sound. The roses round the cottage windows had stopped being roses. They were iron roses now, orange and flaking, and the rust was climbing towards the kitchen door.

Leo stood on the doorstep and watched it come, and felt his heart thudding in his ears.

*If the rust reaches the house, open the chest.*

*The key is where the lions sleep.*

He went to the fireplace. The two iron lions lay curled on the hearth, heads on their paws, eyes closed. He lifted the left one. Nothing. He lifted the right one — it was so heavy he needed both hands — and there, underneath it, lay a long iron key.

* * *

The oak chest opened with a groan.

Inside, there was a folded blanket, a bundle of old letters tied with string, and something long wrapped in oilcloth.

Leo lifted it out. It was heavier than it looked. He peeled the cloth away.

It was a sword.

It was not the kind of sword from films, all shining silver and jewels. It was plain grey steel, a little longer than Leo's arm, with small nicks along its edges, as though it had been used, long ago, for something hard. The grip was wrapped in leather worn dark and smooth by other people's hands. And the pommel — the round weight at the end of the handle — was made in the shape of a lion's head, its mane flowing out around it, its eyes closed. Asleep.

Leo closed his fingers around the grip.

It was warm. Not hot, like metal left in the sun — warm like a mug of tea on a cold morning, warm like a hand holding yours. And along the flat of the blade, so faint that at first he thought he was imagining it, a line of letters glowed for a moment and then faded. They were in a writing Leo had never seen before, all loops and sharp angles.

The lion's eyelids flickered.

Leo nearly dropped it.

Behind him, Biscuit whined. The old dog was standing at the little black door with his nose to the keyhole and his tail low.

Leo looked at the sword. He looked at the note in his other hand. *Go as far from the forge as you can.* That was what Grandad wanted. That would be sensible. That would be *safe*.

He thought about the rust climbing the walls of the cottage. He thought about the crumbling hammer, and the words on the anvil, and Grandad's face when he'd said *some things are my job to worry about*. He thought: *if I run away, who's going to fix this?*

He thought: *Grandad would come for me.*

He was so frightened his teeth were chattering. He walked to the little door anyway.

He looked at the strange keyhole — round, with notches all around its edge, like a flower. Or a mane.

He looked at the lion's head on the end of the sword.

Slowly, he turned the sword round, and pressed the lion's head into the keyhole. It fitted perfectly.

He turned it.

Something clicked, deep inside the wall. The little black door swung open, and warm golden light poured through it — and with the light came the smell of grass and honey, and the sound, impossible, from inside a solid wall, of birdsong.

## Chapter Three: The Little Door

Leo had to duck to get through. Biscuit went first, pushing past his legs, and Leo followed with the sword held out in front of him — and then he was standing on a hillside in the sunshine.

He turned round. Behind him, where the forge should have been, there was only an arch of old grey stones standing on its own in the grass, with ivy growing up its sides. Through the arch he could see the dim inside of Grandad's forge, like a picture in a frame. All around the arch, there was nothing but hill.

He turned back.

The hill rolled down into a valley so green it almost hurt to look at. A river wound through it, flashing silver. There were woods of trees far bigger than any Leo had ever seen, with leaves in every colour from lime to bronze, and beyond the woods rose mountains, blue and white and very far away. It had been lunchtime in Hollowford, but here the sky was the pale gold of early morning. The air smelled of honey and wild thyme and woodsmoke.

And to the north, along the horizon, there was a stain.

It looked like a bruise on the land — a dull, reddish-brown smudge where the hills should have been green, with a grey haze hanging over it. Leo looked at it and felt the hairs on his arms stand up.

"Well," said a gruff voice beside him. "That's torn it."

Leo screamed, jumped, tripped over his own feet, and sat down hard on the grass.

Biscuit looked at him. His bushy eyebrows went up.

"Oh, don't make a fuss," said Biscuit.

"You —" said Leo. "You — you *talked*."

"I always talk."

"You DON'T!"

"I always talk," said Biscuit patiently. "You've just never been on the right side of the door to hear it. Eleven years I've been telling you to stop giving me your crusts. I don't like crusts. Nobody ever listens." He sat down and scratched behind his ear with a back leg. "Now, are you going to sit there with your mouth open like a codfish, or are we going to find your grandad?"

Leo closed his mouth. Then he opened it again. "It was you," he said. "The other night. In the forge. Grandad was talking to *you*."

"Course it was me. Who else would he talk to at two in the morning? The anvil?" Biscuit sniffed. "He wanted to tell you everything. I said you were too young. I was wrong, as it happens." He gave the sword a sideways look. "Mostly I'm not."

Leo got slowly to his feet. His legs felt like jelly. "Where *are* we?"

"Everhallow," said Biscuit. "The other side of the door. Your family have been keeping that door for longer than anyone can remember — you Smiths. Keepers of the door, and keepers of *that*." He nodded at the sword.

"The sword?"

"The sword. The Magic Sword, they call it round here, because nobody round here has got any imagination." Biscuit set off down the hill with his nose to the ground. "It was made on this side, a long time ago, by the first Smith. There's a whole story. You'll have to ask somebody who isn't a dog."

Leo hurried after him. "Where's Grandad?"

Biscuit stopped and snuffled at the grass. The fur along his back rose up. "Came this way. Two days ago, I'd say — time runs differently on this side of the door. Faster or slower, I can never remember which. He wasn't alone." He sniffed again, and his lip curled back from his teeth. "Iron. Old iron. And something rotten." He lifted his head and looked north, at the bruise on the horizon. "They took him that way."

"Who did?"

"The Rust King's lot, I'd bet my dinner on it."

Leo's mouth had gone dry. "Who's the Rust King?"

"Trouble," said Biscuit. "That's who. Where he goes, iron rusts and things fall apart. He's been sitting up there in the north for years and years, spreading and spreading, and your grandad's been worrying about him for just as long." He looked up at Leo. "Your grandad told you to run, didn't he? In that note."

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"No."

Biscuit looked at him for a long moment. His eyes, under their bushy brows, were brown and old and kind.

"Hm," he said. "Maybe you're a Smith after all."

"I'm not brave," said Leo quickly. "I'm really not. I'm scared of — well. Most things. My knees are shaking right now. Look."

"I can see," said Biscuit. "Come on, then. Shaky knees still walk."

* * *

They followed a path down into the valley. Leo had no belt to hang the sword from, so he carried it in his hand, and before long his arm ached so much that he had to keep swapping sides.

But Everhallow was so beautiful that, for a while, he almost forgot to be scared. The path wound through a meadow full of flowers he didn't know the names of — blue ones that chimed very faintly when the breeze moved them, and yellow ones that turned their faces to follow him as he walked past. A bird as bright as a kingfisher, with three long tail feathers that trailed behind it like ribbons, shot past his nose and away. They crossed a stream on stepping stones, and Biscuit fell in, and pretended he had meant to.

But every time they came to the top of a rise, Leo could see the stain in the north a little more clearly. And every time he looked at it, it seemed a little bigger.

Late in the afternoon, the path led into a wood, and Biscuit stopped dead.

He sniffed. He growled, very low.

From somewhere in the trees came a sound — a long, grinding, screeching howl, like a rusty gate being dragged open.

"Leo," said Biscuit quietly. "Get that sword up."

## Chapter Four: Scrapjaws

There were three of them.

They came out of the trees slowly, the way wolves do in nature programmes, heads low, spreading out to surround them. They were the size of wolves and the shape of wolves, but they were not wolves. They were made of scrap: old nails and bent hinges, bits of chain and broken cogs, horseshoes and bolts and the rusted blades of old saws, all crammed and twisted together into the shape of an animal. Their joints screeched when they moved. Their teeth were rusty nails. And in each of their chests, behind a cage of metal ribs, glowed a hot orange coal, like a heart made of fire. Their eyes glowed the same colour.

"Scrapjaws," growled Biscuit. "The Rust King's hounds."

Leo lifted the sword. It was heavy — much heavier than it had been a minute ago, or so it felt — and the tip wobbled all over the place. He waited for it to glow, the way swords did in stories. It didn't. It was just a grey old sword in the hands of a boy whose whole body was shaking.

The biggest Scrapjaw lowered its head and made a noise like a drill hitting a nail.

*Do something,* Leo told himself. *Do something, do something —*

He couldn't. He couldn't move at all. His feet had turned to stone.

The big Scrapjaw sprang.

Biscuit leapt in front of Leo, barking — and at the same moment, something went *whizz* through the air and hit the Scrapjaw on the side of the head with a loud *CLANG*. The creature's head spun halfway round, and it crashed sideways into the bracken.

"Don't just STAND there!" shouted a voice. "Hit them!"

There was a girl standing on a boulder at the side of the path. She was a little older than Leo, with a long dark plait, a face full of freckles, a patched brown coat, and boots that looked two sizes too big. She had a slingshot in her hand and was already fitting another stone into it.

"Aim for the coal!" she yelled. "In the chest! Knock the coal out and they fall apart!"

The second Scrapjaw turned towards her. She let fly — *whizz — CLANG* — and the stone struck its chest, and the glowing coal popped out between its ribs and rolled smoking onto the path. The Scrapjaw froze. Then, with a sound like someone emptying a toolbox, it collapsed into a heap of scrap.

The big one was getting up. Its head was on crooked now, which only made it look worse. It shook itself with a horrible grinding shudder and turned its coal-eyes on Leo.

Leo swung the sword.

He missed by a mile. The weight of it pulled him right round, his feet tangled, and he fell flat on his back in the dirt with the sword still clutched in both hands.

The Scrapjaw loomed over him. It opened its jaws. Its breath smelled of hot metal and old ashes. Leo could see the coal glowing in its chest, right above him —

He didn't decide to do it. He didn't feel brave. He was simply so frightened that his arms did something all on their own: they shoved the sword straight up.

The point slid between the Scrapjaw's metal ribs and struck the coal.

There was a hiss, and a puff of smoke, and the orange glow went out like a blown candle. The creature fell to pieces right on top of him. Leo threw up his arms as nails and hinges and cogs came clattering down and bounced off his shoulders.

The third Scrapjaw gave a screech, spun round, and fled into the trees.

Then everything was quiet.

Leo lay in a pile of scrap, breathing very fast, looking up at the sky.

A freckled face appeared above him, upside down.

"That," said the girl, "was the worst sword fighting I have ever seen."

* * *

Her name was Wren, and she had been tracking the Scrapjaws for two days.

"They've been coming further south," she said, as she hauled Leo to his feet. "Hunting. At the start of summer they came to our village at night, with the Rustmen, and took a whole lot of people and marched them north." Her jaw tightened. "My dad was one of them. Tobias. He's the blacksmith."

"I'm sorry," said Leo.

Wren shrugged, the way people shrug when they mind very much. Then she looked at the sword, and her eyes went wide.

"Where did you get *that*?"

"It's my grandad's."

"Your grandad's." She stared at him — at his muddy jeans and his trainers, at his thin arms, at his hair full of rust flakes. "You're the Keeper's grandson? From the Other Side?"

"I suppose so," said Leo. "Do you know my grandad?"

"Everybody knows *of* him. He came through two nights ago, on his own. My gran saw him on the north road, and the Rustmen were waiting for him. It was a trap." She folded her arms. "They took him to Cinder Keep. That's where the Rust King lives."

Leo's stomach dropped into his trainers.

Wren was still looking at the sword. "Is that really it? The Magic Sword?"

"I think so."

"But it's supposed to shine," she said. "In the songs. When the Keeper lifts it, it blazes like a star, and the darkness runs away screaming." She peered at it. "That's just... a sword. A bit of a rubbish one, too. It's all nicked."

Leo felt his face going hot. He didn't know what to say, because she was right. The sword hadn't blazed. *He* hadn't blazed. He'd fallen over on his bottom in the dirt.

"Manners, girl," rumbled Biscuit.

Wren jumped a foot in the air. She looked at Biscuit. She looked at Leo.

"Your dog talks," she said.

"Apparently," said Leo.

"Dogs don't talk."

"This one does," said Biscuit, "and this one has a sore paw and would like his dinner. So if the two of you have quite finished arguing about swords, perhaps we could get somewhere with a roof."

Wren closed her mouth. Then — surprisingly — she grinned. It changed her whole face.

"All right," she said. "Kettleby's not far. You'd better meet my gran. She'll want to see that sword. Even if it is rubbish."

## Chapter Five: Kettleby

Kettleby was supposed to be noisy.

"It's a smiths' village," Wren said, as they came down the hill in the evening light. "Everyone makes something. Pots, nails, bells, horseshoes. Normally you can hear it for miles — hammer, hammer, hammer, all day long. My dad's forge is the loudest." She stopped at the top of the lane. "Was."

The village was silent.

It was built along both banks of a river, with an old stone bridge in the middle, and little crooked houses with chimneys and workshops attached. But the chimneys were cold. The workshops were shut. The bell in the little tower above the bridge hung silent, its bronze gone green and orange with rust. Rust was everywhere: on the door hinges and the window latches, on the weathervanes and the garden gates, on the ploughs left out in the fields.

And there were the people.

There weren't many about, and the ones who were moved slowly, the way people move when they're very tired. An old man sat on a bench outside his house, staring at nothing. A woman stood in a doorway with a broom in her hands, not sweeping. And there was something wrong with their colours. Leo couldn't work out what it was at first. Then he realised: they were *faded*. Their clothes, their hair, even their skin looked washed out and dim, like an old photograph left too long in a sunny window.

"It's the Greying," said Wren quietly. "It comes with the rust. First you get tired. Then you get sad. Then you stop caring about anything at all, and your colour goes, and you just... sit." She swallowed. "Half the village has got it now. It gets worse every week."

Leo thought of Grandad's tools crumbling into red flakes. He held the sword a little tighter.

* * *

Wren's gran was called Old Mother Tansy, and she lived in the last house before the bridge, in a room so full of things it was like standing inside a magpie's nest. There were bundles of herbs hanging from the beams, and jars of buttons and beads, and half-finished knitting on every chair. Tansy herself was tiny — smaller than Leo — with a face as brown and wrinkled as a walnut, one bright black eye and one cloudy white one, and a voice like a creaky gate.

She did not seem at all surprised to see a talking dog.

"Biscuit," she said. "You've got fat."

"You've got old," said Biscuit.

"I was always old." She turned her one good eye on Leo. It travelled slowly up and down him, and then settled on the sword. "Well. Let's have a look, then."

Leo handed it over. Tansy laid it flat across her knees and bent so close that the tip of her nose nearly touched the steel. She ran one crooked finger along the blade — and as she did, the faint letters Leo had seen in the forge glimmered into view again, like fish rising to the surface of a pond.

"Old Smith-tongue," Tansy murmured. "Haven't seen it written in sixty years." She traced the letters one by one. "*What is broken,*" she read slowly, "*I make whole.*"

There was a silence.

"That's a funny thing for a sword to say," said Leo.

Tansy chuckled — a dry, rattly sound. "Is it? Sit down, boy. Wren, put the kettle on. It's time somebody told this child a story."

* * *

So Leo sat by the fire, with a mug of something hot and sweet that tasted of apples and cinnamon, and Biscuit's warm, heavy head resting on his feet, and Tansy told him.

"Long ago," she said, "long before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, there was a war in Everhallow. The worst war there ever was. Kings fought kings, and villages fought villages, and in the end it wasn't even about anything any more. People just fought because they'd forgotten how to stop. The land cracked. Rivers burst their banks. Whole forests fell. We call it the Breaking.

"And in the middle of all that, there was a smith. Just an ordinary smith, in an ordinary village, a lot like this one. The kings came to her one by one and said, *Make me a sword. Make me a sword so sharp and so strong that I can win.* And she said no. She said no to every single one of them.

"Instead, she went into her forge, and she worked for a year and a day, and she made a different kind of sword. She put into it every mended thing she had ever made — every patched pot, every rejoined chain, every horseshoe she'd ever straightened. And when it was done, she walked out into the middle of the last great battle, and she didn't swing it at anybody. She knelt down, and she pressed it into the broken ground."

Tansy's good eye glittered in the firelight.

"And the ground mended. And the rivers went back into their beds. And the soldiers on both sides looked at each other, and all at once they couldn't remember what they'd been fighting for.

"That smith was the first Smith. Your great-great-great — oh, add as many greats as you like — your grandmother. Any fool can make a sword that breaks things, boy. It takes a Smith to make one that mends."

Leo looked down at the plain, notched blade lying across Tansy's knees. It looked different to him now. Not grander. Just... more.

"Her family have kept it ever since," Tansy went on. "On the Other Side, mostly, where it's safe. And it chooses its Keeper — it always has. It won't shine for just anybody." She peered at him. "It shone for your grandad."

"And the Rust King?" said Leo. "Who is he?"

"Nobody knows," said Tansy. "He came about fifty years ago. Moved into the ruins of Cinder Keep, up in the north, where the worst of the Breaking happened. There's old, bad magic in the stones up there, and he woke it. Where he walks, iron rusts, and hearts go grey. He's been spreading ever since. Slowly at first. Faster, lately."

"What does he want?"

Tansy was quiet for a moment.

"The sword," she said. "He's always wanted the sword. Some say it's because it's the only thing that can undo him, and he wants to destroy it. Some say he wants to use it." She tapped one finger on the lion's closed eyes. "Me? I've always thought he just wants to hold it. I've always thought he wants it to shine for him."

* * *

Later, when Wren was banking up the fire and Biscuit was snoring, Leo said the thing he'd been wanting to say all evening.

"I think it made a mistake."

Tansy looked up from her knitting.

"The sword," said Leo. His voice came out small. "It glowed a tiny bit when I first picked it up. But it hasn't since. Not properly. Not when the Scrapjaws came. I just froze, and Wren had to save me, and then I fell over." He stared into the fire. "I'm not brave. Everyone at school knows it. I'm scared of everything. Grandad should have left it to somebody else."

Tansy put down her knitting.

"Brave?" she said. "Who said anything about brave? The sword doesn't care if you're brave, boy. It doesn't care if you're big, or strong, or clever, or good at hitting things. It only cares about one thing."

"What?"

"What you *do*," said Tansy, and picked up her knitting again, as though that settled it.

* * *

Leo couldn't sleep. After a while he gave up, climbed out of the little attic window where Tansy had made up a bed for him, and sat on the roof looking at the stars. There were far more of them in Everhallow than at home, and none of them were in the right places.

The window creaked. Wren climbed out and sat down beside him with her knees pulled up under her chin.

For a while, neither of them said anything.

"Sorry I said your sword was rubbish," said Wren at last.

"It's all right. It sort of is."

"It isn't really. I was just cross." She picked at a loose thread on her coat. "I've been cross all summer. Ever since they took Dad. Everyone in the village said there was nothing we could do. Gran said to wait for the Keeper. So I waited. And then the Keeper came, and they took *him* as well, and —" She stopped. "I was going to go to Cinder Keep on my own. When I found you. I was going to get Dad back by myself."

"That's really brave," said Leo.

Wren gave a short laugh. "I'm scared out of my mind. I've been scared for weeks. I just don't tell anyone."

Leo looked at her in surprise. She had seemed so fearless on that boulder, with her slingshot, shouting at him.

"I'm scared all the time," he admitted. "All the time. Right now, even."

"Then why did you come through the door?"

Leo thought about it.

"Because Grandad would have come for me," he said.

Wren was quiet for a long time. Then she dug in her pocket and pulled out something wrapped in a handkerchief: two squashed, slightly fluffy oatcakes. She gave him one.

"Gran's drawing us a map," she said, with her mouth full. "North through the Whispering Wood. Across the Nothing Gorge on the Old Bridge. Then over the Greywaste to Cinder Keep. Three days, she says."

"Us?" said Leo.

"Well, you're not going on your own. You'd fall over." She grinned in the starlight. "Two days, if you can keep up."

## Chapter Six: The Whispering Wood

Tansy saw them off at dawn. She gave them a map drawn on the back of an old recipe, a bag of oatcakes and cheese and apples, a proper leather scabbard and belt for the sword ("It was my father's. It'll do."), and a warning.

"The Whispering Wood knows what you're afraid of," she said, looking hard at each of them in turn with her one good eye. "It'll say it out loud. It'll say it over and over. Don't stop walking, whatever you hear. The ones who stop never come out."

Leo buckled the belt round his waist. The sword hung at his side, bumping against his leg. It made him feel a little bit like someone in a story, and a lot like someone who was about to be in trouble.

The Whispering Wood began at the top of the valley, and it was beautiful. That was the first surprise. Leo had expected something dark and twisted, but the trees were tall and slender, with silvery bark and leaves so pale they were almost white, and the light that came through them was soft and pearly. It was very quiet. There were no birds.

The leaves rustled.

It was a gentle sound, at first. Like wind — except that there wasn't any wind. Like whispering — except that there was nobody there.

Then Leo began to hear words.

*...leo...*

He stopped. Wren grabbed his arm and pulled him on. "Keep walking."

*...little leo... little leo the lion...*

The whispers came from everywhere, from every leaf on every tree. Soft voices, sly voices, voices that sounded almost like people he knew.

*...scared of the dark... scared of dogs... scared of deep water... scared of the pritchard twins... scared of everything...*

Leo walked faster. His face was burning.

*...froze when the scrapjaws came... fell over in the dirt... the girl had to save you...*

*...the sword made a mistake...*

*...grandad made a mistake...*

Beside him, Wren was walking with her jaw clenched and her eyes fixed straight ahead. Leo could hear her whispers too, underneath his own — different words, in different voices.

*...too late, wren... you're too late... he's already grey... he won't even know you... you're always too late...*

And from somewhere down by his knees came a third set of whispers.

*...old dog... slow dog... couldn't catch a squirrel if it had a broken leg...*

"That," muttered Biscuit, "is *slander*."

They kept walking. Leo didn't know for how long. The path wound between the silver trees, and the whispers followed them, louder and louder, until it felt as though they were inside his own head.

*...you'll fail... you'll get there and you'll freeze and they'll take the sword and it'll be your fault...*

*...grandad will turn to rust and it'll be your fault...*

*...you should have run... you should have run like he told you...*

Then Leo noticed the tree.

It had a crooked branch, low down, that looked like a bent elbow. He had seen it before. He was sure he had seen it before.

A little further on, he saw it again.

"Wren," he said. "We're going round in circles."

Wren didn't answer. Leo turned round.

She had stopped walking. She was standing in the middle of the path with her hands pressed over her ears and her eyes screwed shut, and tears were running down her freckled cheeks.

*...too late... too late... too late...*

"Wren! Keep walking! Your gran said —"

"I can't," she whispered. "I can't, I can't, it's true, it's all true —"

And the light was going. The pearly light between the trees was fading to grey, and then to dusk, and then, far faster than any real evening, to dark. A thick, total, swallowing dark. The dark from the cupboard under the stairs. The dark from Leo's room in Grandad's roof. The dark he was more afraid of than anything else in any world.

Leo couldn't see Wren. He couldn't see his own hands. He could only hear the whispers, pouring out of the blackness on every side.

*...scared of the dark... scared of the dark... little leo is scared of the dark...*

And Leo, standing there with his heart hammering so hard he thought it might crack his ribs, thought: *Yes. I am.*

It was true. Every single thing the trees had said was true. He *was* scared of the dark. He *had* frozen. He *had* fallen over. He might fail. He might get all the way to Cinder Keep and be too frightened to do anything at all.

Tansy had said *don't stop walking*. She had never said *don't answer*.

"Yes," Leo said out loud.

His voice sounded very small in the dark. He said it again, louder.

"Yes. You're right. I'm scared of the dark. I'm scared of the Scrapjaws, and the Rust King, and I'm scared of that bridge, and I'm scared I'm going to get there and mess everything up." His voice was shaking, but he kept going. "I'm scared I'm not good enough. I'm scared Grandad chose wrong. I'm *scared*, all right? I'm really, really scared."

He took a breath.

"And I'm going anyway."

The whispers stopped.

Just like that — as if a door had closed. The wood went completely silent.

And at Leo's side, something began to glow.

It was not a blaze. It was nothing like a star. It was a soft, warm, golden light, like a candle in a window, like the glow of coals when a fire has burned down low, and it was coming from the sword. Leo drew it from the scabbard with a trembling hand, and the light spread out around him in a circle — just enough to see his own feet, and the path, and the silver tree trunks, and Wren, still standing with her hands over her ears, blinking in the light.

On the pommel, the lion's eyes had opened. Only a crack — but they were open, and they were gold.

Leo held out his free hand.

"Say it," he said to Wren. "Say it back to them. It works."

Wren looked at him. Her face was blotchy from crying. For a moment he thought she wouldn't.

Then she took his hand, and gripped it hard, and lifted her chin at the dark trees.

"I'm scared I'm too late," she said loudly. "I'm scared he won't know me. I'm going anyway."

Down by their feet, Biscuit cleared his throat. "I am a bit slow," he said, with enormous dignity. "I'm eleven. That's seventy-seven in dog years, or something. I've never understood the maths. I'm going anyway."

The wood stayed silent.

And in the light of the sword, the path, which had been curling round and round in circles, simply straightened out, and ran north, straight as a ruler, between the trees.

* * *

They came out of the Whispering Wood as the real sun was rising, and all three of them sat down in the long grass at its edge, and for a while nobody said anything at all.

The sword had faded back to ordinary grey. But when Leo held it, it was still warm.

"It glowed," said Wren at last. "It actually glowed."

"Only a bit."

"It *glowed*, Leo." She shoved his shoulder. "For you."

Leo looked down at the lion's head on the pommel. Its eyes were closed again. But he was almost sure — almost — that it was smiling.

"I didn't feel brave," he said. "I thought you had to feel brave."

Biscuit rolled over onto his back in the grass, wriggled, and sighed with happiness.

"Nobody ever *feels* brave, lad," he said to the sky. "That's all courage ever is. Being scared, and going anyway. Anyone who tells you different is selling something."

## Chapter Seven: The Bridge Over Nothing

Leo smelled the Nothing Gorge before he saw it: a cold, damp smell, like the inside of a cave. Then the grass simply ended, and there was the edge.

He stopped a good five steps away from it. Wren walked right up and peered over, which made Leo's stomach turn a somersault.

"Come and look," she said.

"I'm fine here, thanks."

The gorge was a gash in the earth, as if a giant had split the land with an axe. It was wider than a football pitch. The far side was a cliff of grey stone, and below, instead of a river or rocks, there was only mist — thick, white, rolling mist, with no bottom that anyone could see. If you dropped a stone into it, Wren said, you'd never hear it land.

Leo decided not to test this.

The Old Bridge stood a little way along the edge. Once, Leo could tell, it must have been magnificent: three great arches of carved stone, with enormous iron chains strung along each side for railings, every link as big as Leo's head. A stone lion sat at either end, keeping watch.

But the rust had got to it. The chains were eaten away to orange lace. The stones were cracked and crumbling. And in the middle, where the central arch should have been, there was just... a gap. A gap as wide as a road, with a few rusty chains dangling down into the mist, swaying gently in the cold wind.

"Oh," said Wren. "Gran's map is a bit out of date."

"Is there another way across?" said Leo, very hopefully.

Wren unfolded the recipe-map and studied it. "Not for about two weeks' walk."

Behind them, from the edge of the Whispering Wood, came a long, grinding screech.

They spun round. At the edge of the trees, Scrapjaws were gathering. Not three this time. Leo counted seven — eight — ten. And he was sure the one at the front was the one that had run away.

"It went and got its friends," said Biscuit grimly.

The Scrapjaws began to trot towards them across the grass, joints squealing, coal-eyes glowing.

Wren loaded her slingshot. Her hands were shaking. "I haven't got enough stones for ten."

"We could jump," said Leo. It came out as a squeak.

"It's *way* too far."

"I know. I don't know why I said it."

The Scrapjaws were breaking into a run. Leo backed away towards the bridge, his heart hammering, until his heel bumped against something — the broken end of one of the great iron chains, lying across the stones, flaking with rust.

He looked down at it.

It was broken. It was only broken.

And a broken thing, Leo thought, was just a thing waiting for somebody to be patient with it.

He didn't really know what he was doing. He drew the sword — it came up warm into his hand — and he knelt down and laid the flat of the blade against the rusted chain.

*What is broken, I make whole.*

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the rust began to lift. It flaked off the chain like dry leaves blowing away, and underneath, the iron was dark and bright and new. The brightness ran along the chain, away from the sword, link after link, like a flame running along a fuse. The chain lifted itself off the ground. It stretched out into the air above the gorge, and the dangling links on the far side came swinging up to meet it, and they joined with a sound like a bell. The cracked stones along the bridge ground together and sealed. The middle arch rose up out of the mist, stone by stone, fitting itself together like a jigsaw puzzle doing itself.

In ten seconds, there was a bridge.

"Leo," breathed Wren. "*Leo.*"

"I know," said Leo. "I saw."

But the moment he lifted the sword even a little way from the chain, the bright iron in the middle began to dim, and a thin line of orange crept back along it. He pressed the blade down again, and the rust fell back.

"It only holds while I'm touching it," he said. "I think I'm rushing it. It's like sticky tape. It needs longer to mend properly."

"We haven't *got* longer!" Wren fired her slingshot — *CLANG* — and the leading Scrapjaw stumbled. The others kept coming.

Leo looked at the bridge. He looked at the mist beneath it. He thought about walking across that, over nothing, over a drop with no bottom.

*Being scared, and going anyway.*

"Go!" he shouted. "Both of you, go! I'll be right behind you!"

Wren and Biscuit ran onto the bridge. Leo went after them, walking sideways, keeping the flat of the sword pressed against the great chain that ran along the side like a handrail. Wherever the sword touched, the iron shone, and the bridge held firm.

He made the mistake of looking down.

Below the edge of the bridge there was nothing but white, swirling mist, going down and down forever. His head swam. His legs went weak. For one horrible moment the whole world seemed to tip sideways, and he thought he was going to be sick, or fall, or both.

"Don't look down!" yelled Wren from up ahead.

"Bit late!" Leo yelled back.

He made himself breathe. In and out. He made himself look — really look — at the mist, and at the drop, and at how scared he was. Then he took another step.

And another.

He was nearly at the middle when the Scrapjaws reached the bridge. He heard their feet clanging on the stone behind him, and their horrible grinding howls, getting closer.

*Don't run,* he told himself. *If you run, you'll lift the sword, and the bridge will go.*

So he didn't run. He walked. Step by step, sword to the chain, the whole bridge shining ahead of him, with ten Scrapjaws coming up behind. It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

Wren and Biscuit reached the far side. Leo came after them, with the Scrapjaws only a few metres behind, and the moment both his feet were on solid ground, he lifted the sword away from the chain.

The quick mending let go.

It went all at once. The shining iron dimmed to orange; the orange swept across the middle arch in a rush, like a wave; and the stones and chains, which had only been holding together because Leo had asked them to, remembered that they were broken. With a long, groaning rumble, the middle of the bridge crumbled away and fell into the mist, and ten Scrapjaws went with it.

Leo waited. He listened.

He never heard them land.

* * *

Wren sat down very suddenly on the grass.

"It *mends* things," she said. "Leo. The Magic Sword *mends* things. The songs never said that. The songs are all *blazing* and *smiting* and *the darkness fled before it*. Nobody ever wrote a song about it fixing a *bridge*."

"Maybe the people who wrote the songs wanted a sword that hit things," said Leo. He was sitting down too. His legs didn't seem to want to hold him up any more.

"What's wrong with hitting things?"

"Nothing. Probably. It's just..." Leo looked back at the broken bridge. "Anyone can hit things," he said.

He looked at the gap for a long time, and at the two stone lions at either end, sitting there, rusting in the wind.

"I'm going to come back and mend it properly," he said. "One day. Slowly. So it lasts."

Biscuit licked his ear. "Course you are," he said. "You're a Smith."

## Chapter Eight: The Greywaste

Beyond the Nothing Gorge, the colour went out of the world.

It happened gradually. First the grass turned brown, then orange, then crisp and metallic — rust-grass, that crunched and snapped under their feet. The trees were iron trees, orange and flaking, their branches creaking in the wind like old hinges. The sky itself went dim, as though someone had pulled a grey cloth across it. There were no birds, no insects, no flowers. Just rust, and grey dust, and a thin, cold wind that never stopped.

"The Greywaste," said Wren quietly. "It used to be farms, Gran says. Orchards. The best apples in Everhallow."

On the horizon, a little closer with every hour, stood Cinder Keep.

It was a tower — one enormous tower of black stone, rising out of the waste like a burnt finger, streaked orange from top to bottom where the rust had run down it like rain. At the very top, a single window glowed a dull, sullen red, like the last coal in a dying fire.

Leo looked at it and felt the Greying tug at him. It was hard to describe. It was like a weight on his chest, a voice that didn't whisper like the trees but just sort of sighed: *what's the point? What's the point of any of it?* His feet got heavier. Walking got harder. And when he looked at Wren, he saw that even her freckles seemed a little fainter.

When it got too bad, he put his hand on the sword. It was still warm. It helped.

They walked all day. Biscuit's paws got sore on the rust-grass, and Leo carried him for a while, which Biscuit pretended to hate. They ate the last of Tansy's cheese in the shelter of a dead iron tree. And then, as the grey afternoon was turning into grey evening, Wren grabbed Leo's arm and pulled him down behind a rock.

"Look," she breathed.

Below them, a road ran towards Cinder Keep, and along the road came a line of people.

There were dozens of them, pulling carts piled high with scrap metal: broken ploughs, bent swords, old pans and gates and wheels. They walked slowly, heads down, shuffling. Every one of them was grey — completely grey, from their hair to their boots, as grey as statues. Around them marched guards: Scrapjaws, and something else, something Leo hadn't seen before. They looked like knights in full armour, tall and broad, but the armour was red with rust, and when one of them turned its head, Leo saw that there was nothing inside the helmet at all. Just darkness.

"Rustmen," whispered Wren. "They're empty. Just armour, walking."

Leo shivered.

Then Wren went very still.

"That's my dad," she said.

Near the back of the line, pulling one of the heaviest carts, was a huge man with a thick beard and shoulders like a barn door. He would have looked every inch a blacksmith, if he hadn't been the colour of ashes.

Wren was already half on her feet. Leo grabbed her coat and dragged her back down.

"Let me GO —"

"Wren, there are about fifty Rustmen down there!"

"That's my *dad*!"

"I know! I know. But if you run down there now, they'll take you too." Leo held on tight. "Wait till dark. Please. We'll get to him. I promise."

Wren glared at him with wet, furious eyes. Then, slowly, she stopped pulling.

* * *

The line of workers made camp at nightfall beside a ruined farmhouse — if you could call it a camp. The grey people simply stopped where they were and lay down in the dust, and the Rustmen stood around them in a ring, not moving, not sleeping, their empty helmets turned outwards.

It was Biscuit who found the way in. "Ditch," he muttered. "Round the back. Stinks of old turnips. They won't be looking there."

So they crawled along a ditch that stank of old turnips, on their bellies, in the dark, with the Rustmen's empty helmets only a few metres away. Leo's heart was thumping so hard he was sure they'd hear it. At the end of the ditch they slipped between two grey figures lying in the dust, and crept to the place where Wren's father lay on his back, staring up at the sky with open eyes.

"Dad," Wren whispered. She shook his shoulder. "Dad, it's me. It's Wren."

The big man turned his head very slowly and looked at her. There was nothing in his face at all. No surprise, no happiness. Nothing.

"Go home, girl," he said. His voice was flat and grey, like everything else. "There's no point. There's no point in anything."

Wren made a small sound, as if something inside her had cracked.

Leo drew the sword — just a few centimetres, just enough to shine — and very gently laid the flat of the blade against the back of the big man's grey hand.

Colour flooded into it.

It spread up his wrist and along his arm like dye soaking into cloth — the brown of his skin, the blue of his shirt, the red of his beard. His eyes cleared and brightened. He blinked. He looked at Wren, and his whole face changed.

"*Wren?*" he whispered. "Wren, love? What are you — how did you —"

"Dad!" Wren flung her arms round his neck.

But even as she did, Leo saw the grey begin to creep back. It came from outside, like cold coming in through an open window — seeping up out of the ground, sinking down out of the air, flowing, Leo realised, from the direction of the tower. He pressed the sword harder against Tobias's hand, and the colour held for a moment, and then began to fade again anyway.

"It's no good," Leo said. "It's like fixing a leak in a bucket while it's still under a waterfall. The grey's coming from somewhere. I can't mend it faster than it comes back."

"It comes from *him*," said Tobias. He was holding on to Wren as if she might blow away, and speaking fast, while he still could. "From the Keep. From the Rust King himself. He's the source of it — it pours off him like smoke off a fire." He looked at Leo, and at the sword, and his eyes went wide. "You're the boy. The Keeper's boy."

"His grandson," said Leo. "Is he — is Grandad —"

"He's alive. They've got him chained up in the great hall, next to the old forge. The Rust King goes down to him every night." Tobias's voice was fading, going flat again, as the grey rose up his neck. "We can hear him from the workshops. Shouting. Shouting the same thing, over and over."

"Shouting what?"

Tobias's eyes were dimming. He struggled to get the words out.

"*Why you,*" he said. "*Why you, and not me.* And he calls him..." His face was almost grey again now. "He calls the Keeper... *brother*."

The last of the colour drained out of him. His eyes went dull, and he turned his face slowly back to the sky.

Wren held on to him and cried without making a sound.

And Leo sat back on his heels in the grey dust, with the sword in his hand, and thought about a faded black-and-white photograph on Grandad's mantelpiece. Two boys in front of a forge. One small and shy. One taller and older, with his arms folded and his chin up, as if he were daring the whole world to argue with him.

*He went away when we were boys.*

"Edwin," Leo whispered.

## Chapter Nine: Cinder Keep

In the morning, the line of grey workers set off towards the tower again, and Leo, Wren and Biscuit followed at a distance, keeping low, keeping to the ditches. Wren hadn't said much since the night before. Her eyes were red, but her jaw was set.

By midday, they were lying flat on a ridge of rust, looking down at Cinder Keep.

Close up, it was even worse. The black tower rose so high that its top was lost in the grey haze. Around its base ran a wall, and in the wall was a gate — a huge gate of iron bars, rusted and dripping. Rustmen stood along the wall, still as statues. Scrapjaws prowled in the yard. Smoke rose from chimneys at the tower's foot, where the workshops were — where, Tobias had said, the grey workers melted down scrap to make more Rustmen, and more Scrapjaws, and more rust.

"There's a drain," said Wren, pointing. "See? Where the water comes out of the workshops, at the bottom of the wall. It'll be disgusting, but we could get in that way. Sneak in, find your grandad, find the keys..."

Leo was looking at the gate.

"He's waiting for me," he said slowly.

"What?"

"The Rust King. He's been waiting for the sword for fifty years, your gran said. He sent the rust through the door to get Grandad over here. But Grandad didn't bring the sword — he left it locked up, for me. So the Rust King's sitting in there with Grandad, just *waiting*, because he knows the sword will come." Leo swallowed. "He'll be expecting us to sneak in. He'll have the drain watched, and every window, and every door. The only thing he won't expect is somebody walking straight up to the front gate."

Wren stared at him. "You want to walk up to the *front gate*?"

"Not *want*," said Leo. "Definitely not want."

"He'll take the sword off you!"

"Maybe." Leo's hands were cold. "But while he's busy with me, everyone will be looking at me. Nobody will be watching the drain. You and Biscuit could get into the workshops. Find your dad and the others. Find the keys to Grandad's chains."

"That's mad," said Wren. "That's the maddest plan I've ever heard."

"I know."

"You're scared of the *dark*, Leo."

"I know," said Leo. "I'm scared of the dark, and of heights, and of dogs —"

"Present company excepted, I hope," said Biscuit.

"— and of the Pritchard twins, and I'm *really* scared of the Rust King. I'm going anyway."

Wren looked at him for a long time.

Then she did something he didn't expect. She hugged him — quickly and fiercely, so hard his ribs creaked.

"Don't you dare get rusted," she said into his shoulder.

"I'll try not to."

Biscuit pushed his big shaggy head under Leo's hand. "You're a Smith, lad," he said gruffly. "Remember that, whatever happens in there. Your grandad's a Smith, and his brother's a Smith, and you're a Smith. And do you know what Smiths do?"

"Make things?"

"Mend things," said Biscuit. "Now go on. Before I get sentimental."

* * *

Leo walked down the ridge alone.

His legs were shaking so badly that he had to think about every step. The rust-grass crunched under his trainers. The tower got bigger and bigger until it filled the whole sky. As he came close, every Rustman on the wall turned its empty helmet towards him, all at once, with a grinding creak. The Scrapjaws in the yard stopped prowling, and pressed themselves against the bars of the gate, and growled.

Leo stopped in front of the gate. He drew the sword and held it out in front of him, point down, where everyone could see it.

It took him three tries before his voice would work.

"My name," he said, "is Leo Smith. I've come to see my great-uncle Edwin."

For a long moment there was no sound but the wind.

Then, with a scream of rusted metal, the gate began to open.

* * *

Inside, Cinder Keep was all iron and shadow. Rustmen marched on either side of Leo as he walked, their empty armour clanking, and led him across a courtyard, up a flight of wide black steps, and through a doorway so tall that he felt like an ant walking into a cathedral.

The great hall was vast and gloomy and cold. Rust hung from the ceiling in long orange icicles. Pillars of black stone rose up into darkness. Along one side of the hall stood a forge — an enormous forge, far bigger than Grandad's, with a hearth like the mouth of a cave. But its fire was out. Its bellows hung in tatters. Its coals were cold and grey.

And in front of the cold forge, beside a huge black anvil, chained to it by both wrists, was Grandad.

He looked terrible. He was dirty and tired, and there were streaks of grey in his face that shouldn't have been there. But when he saw Leo, he surged to his feet so fast his chains rattled.

"Leo! Oh, Leo, you daft, *brave* boy — I told you to run! I told you not to open that door!"

"I know," said Leo. His voice wobbled. He wanted very much to run across the hall and throw his arms round Grandad's middle, but a Rustman's iron hand was on his shoulder. "You also told me anything broken can be mended, if you're patient with it. So I thought I'd better come."

Grandad made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else.

"Well," said a voice from the far end of the hall. "Isn't that touching."

Leo turned.

At the end of the great hall stood a throne built out of scrap — out of swords and shields and chains and cogs and gates, all welded together into a great jagged heap. And on the throne sat a figure in armour so thick with rust that it looked as though it had grown there. He wore a crown of old nails. His face was hidden behind a helmet with a narrow slit for eyes.

When he stood up, flakes of rust fell off him like snow.

He came down from the throne slowly, and rust spread out across the stone floor from every footstep. When he spoke, his voice sounded like a door that hadn't been opened in fifty years.

"So," said the Rust King. "The sword chose again."

He stopped in front of Leo, very close, and looked down at him through the slit in his helmet.

"And *again*," he said, "it wasn't me."

He raised two rust-crusted gauntlets, took hold of his helmet, and lifted it off.

Underneath was an old man.

He was thin — much thinner than Grandad — and his skin was patched with rust, like lichen on an old gravestone. His hair was grey and wild. His eyes were pale, pale grey, and cold as winter. But the shape of his face — the long nose, the heavy brows, the strong chin —

It was Grandad's face. Grandad's face, if Grandad had never once in his whole life laughed.

"Hello, Leo," said Edwin Smith.

## Chapter Ten: The Rust King

"You know who I am," said Edwin. "I can see it on your face. Did Walter tell you about me?"

"No," said Leo. "I worked it out. There's a photo, on the mantelpiece. You and Grandad, in front of the forge."

Something flickered across Edwin's face, too fast to read. "He kept that?"

"I kept everything, Ed," said Grandad quietly, from beside the anvil. "Every letter I wrote you that I had nowhere to send. Every —"

"*Be quiet!*" The shout rang round the great hall, and rust rained down from the ceiling. Edwin was breathing hard. Then, with a visible effort, he made himself calm. He turned back to Leo and smiled a thin, cold smile. "Has Walter ever told you, boy, how the sword chooses its Keeper?"

Leo shook his head.

"It's a family tradition," said Edwin. "When the Keeper's eldest child turns thirteen, the Keeper brings out the sword and lets them hold it. And if it shines, that child will be Keeper next. The eldest. That's how it had always been, as far back as anyone could remember."

He began to pace, and wherever he walked, the rust crept after him.

"I was the eldest. I was the strongest boy in Hollowford. The fastest, and the cleverest, and the bravest. I wasn't scared of *anything*. I climbed the church tower for a dare when I was nine. I fought three boys at once when I was ten, and won. I'd been waiting my whole life to be Keeper — I *practised*, every single day, with a stick in the garden." His voice twisted. "And on my thirteenth birthday, our father brought out the sword and put it in my hands.

"And nothing happened."

The hall was very quiet.

"Nothing at all. Just cold steel." Edwin's pale eyes had gone far away. "And then Walter — little Walter, ten years old, with his sticking-out ears, who cried when he stepped on a snail, who spent all his time mending birds' nests and gluing teacups back together — little Walter asked if he could have a go. And Father laughed, and let him.

"And it *shone*."

He rounded on Grandad, and his voice rose until it cracked.

"It shone like the sun! For *him!* For the boy who was scared of the dark!"

Leo looked at Grandad. Grandad's head was bowed.

"I couldn't bear it," said Edwin. "Everybody was so *pleased*. So that night, I took the sword out of the chest, and I went through the little door. I was going to prove it had made a mistake. I was going to find a monster in Everhallow and slay it, and then the sword would *have* to shine for me. It wouldn't have any choice."

"What happened?" asked Leo, in a small voice.

"I walked for weeks," said Edwin. "I found monsters. I fought them. I won. And the sword never shone for me. Not once. Not a flicker." His hands clenched into fists. "In the end, I threw it into a river and walked away."

"Father found it," said Grandad softly. "Months later. He came through the door looking for you, and he found the sword, but he never found you. None of us ever found you, Ed. I looked. Every summer for fifty years, I came through that door and I looked."

"I didn't want to be found." Edwin turned his back on them both. "I wandered, and I ended up here, in the Greywaste, in these ruins. There's old magic in these stones — bad magic, left over from the Breaking. Rust magic. It was asleep. I was so angry, boy, and so bitter, that my anger woke it up." He held up one hand and looked at it — at the rust crusting his fingers and creeping up his wrist. "After that, everything I touched rusted. Everything went grey. And I thought: *good*. If nothing in this world will shine for me, then *nothing will shine at all*."

He turned round. His eyes were wet and wild.

"Fifty years I've waited. Fifty years building my Keep, and my Rustmen, and spreading the rust inch by inch. And at last, when I was ready, I sent it through the door into Walter's forge, because I knew he'd come. He always was too soft — he could never bear to see anything broken. I thought he'd bring the sword with him. But he's cleverer than he used to be." He looked at Leo. "He left it for *you*. And it chose you. Of course it did. Another little mender. Another scared little boy."

He held out one rust-crusted hand.

"Give it to me," said the Rust King. "Let me hold it. Just once more. Let it shine for me, and I'll let you both go."

Leo looked at the hand. He could have simply taken it, Leo realised. There were a hundred Rustmen in the hall; Edwin could have snatched the sword the moment Leo walked through the gate. But he hadn't. He wanted it *given* to him — the way their father had given it to Walter, all those years ago.

"Leo," said Grandad sharply. "Don't. Don't you dare."

Leo looked at Grandad in his chains, the grey creeping into his face. He thought about Tobias, and the grey workers, and the Greying in Kettleby, and the bridge crumbling into the mist.

And he thought about something Grandad had said in the forge, right at the very beginning. *Iron's like people. Hit it too hard while it's cold and it'll crack on you. Warm it through first.*

He didn't know if it was the right thing to do. He only knew that fighting wasn't going to mend anything.

"Leo, NO —"

Leo turned the sword round, and held it out hilt first, and put it into his great-uncle's hand.

* * *

For a moment, Edwin just stood there holding it, with a look on his face like a starving man looking at bread.

Then the rust began to spread.

It crept out from his fingers onto the leather of the grip. It crept over the lion's head, and the lion's closed eyes. It crept up the blade, orange and flaking, dulling the steel, swallowing the faint letters one by one, until the whole sword was the same dead red-brown as everything else in Cinder Keep.

It didn't shine. Not even a flicker.

Edwin stared at it. His face began to change — from hunger, to disbelief, to a terrible, twisting fury.

"Even now," he whispered. "Even *now*."

"Ed," said Grandad. "Ed, listen to me —"

"*EVEN NOW!*"

Edwin lifted the sword high over his head in both hands and brought it down on the great black anvil with all his strength.

There was a crack like the end of the world.

And the Magic Sword broke in two.

* * *

The blade snapped a hand's width above the hilt. The top half spun away across the floor, ringing, and came to rest at Leo's feet. The hilt, with its stub of blade, fell from Edwin's hands and clattered onto the stone.

For a second, there was no sound at all.

Then Cinder Keep *groaned*. The whole tower shuddered from top to bottom. Rust poured from the ceiling like orange rain. Outside, Leo heard a great roaring rush, like wind or water, and through the tall windows he saw the grey haze over the Greywaste darken and billow and begin to roll outwards — south, towards the Gorge, towards the Whispering Wood, towards Kettleby.

Grandad sank to his knees beside the anvil. The grey was rising up his neck.

And Edwin was laughing. A high, cracked, terrible laugh.

Then, all at once, he stopped.

He looked down at his hands. The rust on them was spreading faster now — up his wrists, up his arms, across his chest, eating into him. He looked at the broken sword on the floor. And suddenly he didn't look like a king at all. He looked like a boy who had broken something precious in a temper, and knew that he could never, ever take it back.

"No," Edwin whispered. "No. That's not — I didn't mean —"

Leo didn't hear the rest.

He was on his knees on the cold stone floor, with the two halves of the Magic Sword in his hands.

## Chapter Eleven: What Is Broken

The pieces were cold.

That was the worst thing. The sword had always been warm, from the very first moment he'd touched it in Grandad's forge. Now the two halves were cold and dull and heavy, crusted with rust, and the lion on the pommel had its eyes squeezed tight shut, as if it couldn't bear to look.

The whole Keep was shaking. Leo could hear Rustmen marching towards the great hall from every direction, their armour clanking. Through the windows, the grey was rolling out across the land like a flood.

It was over. He had given the sword away, and it had been broken, and it was his fault. The whispers in the wood had been right all along.

"Leo."

Grandad's voice. Hoarse and tired, but steady.

Leo looked up. Grandad was on his knees too, grey to the chin, chained to the anvil. But his eyes — his eyes were still Grandad's.

"Leo," he said. "What do I always say?"

Leo's throat was so tight he could hardly speak.

"Anything broken can be mended," he whispered. "If you're patient with it."

"Well, then," said Grandad. And he smiled — the same smile that folded his whole face up like a map. "Be patient with it."

Leo looked down at the two halves of the sword in his hands.

It was broken. It was only broken.

He turned and looked at the enormous cold forge. At its dead grey coals, and its tattered bellows, and its great hearth like the mouth of a cave.

"I need the fire lit," he said.

* * *

The doors at the far end of the hall burst open.

Leo spun round, expecting Rustmen. Instead, in charged Biscuit, barking like a whole pack of dogs, and behind him came Wren — filthy from head to foot and smelling powerfully of drains — with her slingshot in one hand and an enormous ring of rusty keys in the other. And behind *her*, shuffling and slow, came the grey workers, dozens of them, with Tobias at their head.

"Found the keys!" Wren shouted. "And I found everyone else! They're not much use, they keep sitting down, but I told them to follow me and they sort of did —" She saw the broken sword and stopped dead. "Leo. What *happened*?"

"No time," said Leo. "Grandad's chains! And I need the forge lit. Really hot. Hotter than it's ever been."

Wren didn't ask a single question. She ran to the anvil and started trying keys in Grandad's padlocks. Leo ran to the hearth and started dragging coal from the heap beside it in great armfuls, black dust all down his front.

Then the Rustmen arrived.

They came in through every door at once, a clanking tide of empty armour, and behind them came the Scrapjaws. Biscuit planted himself in front of the forge with every hair on end and bared his old yellow teeth. "Come on, then," he growled. "Come and have a go, if you think you're rusty enough."

The first Scrapjaw leapt. Biscuit caught it by the leg and swung it round into a Rustman, and they both went down in a clatter. Wren's slingshot sang — *whizz, CLANG, whizz, CLANG* — and Scrapjaw coals went flying.

The chain round Grandad's left wrist fell away. Then the right.

Grandad rose to his feet — grey and stiff and old, but rising — and put his great hand on Leo's shoulder.

"The bellows," said Leo desperately. "I can't light the fire and work the bellows at the same time, and it needs to be *so* hot —"

"Dad," said Wren.

She had turned to Tobias. He stood among the grey workers, huge and faded, staring at nothing.

"Dad," Wren said again. She took his big grey hand and put it on the handle of the great bellows. "Please. You're the best smith in Kettleby. Everybody says so. We need the fire. *Please.*"

For a long moment, Tobias didn't move.

Then, slowly, his fingers closed round the handle.

He pushed down. The bellows wheezed. He pulled up and pushed down again, harder, and the dead coals in the hearth gave a tiny orange glow.

Leo struck a spark from the flint on the hearth shelf. The coal caught. Tobias worked the bellows — up and down, up and down, faster and faster — and the fire grew and grew until it was roaring, white-hot, lighting up the whole great hall. And as it did, Leo saw that there was a little colour back in Tobias's face. Just a little. The colour of a man doing the thing he loved best.

All around the hall, the other grey workers had turned towards the light. One by one, slowly, they picked up tools — shovels, hammers, pokers — and went to stand between the forge and the Rustmen.

* * *

Leo put both halves of the sword into the fire.

"You'll never do it," said a voice behind him.

It was Edwin. He hadn't moved from the spot where he'd broken the sword. The rust had reached his neck now, and was creeping over his jaw. He looked like a statue someone had left out in the rain for a hundred years.

"You'll never do it," he said again, but there was no anger left in his voice. Only a sort of hopeless wonder. "That blade was forged by the first Smith. Nobody can mend it. Not Walter. Not our father. Not a scared little boy."

Leo didn't answer. He was watching the sword in the fire. The rust was burning away from it. The steel was turning dull red, then cherry, then orange, then a yellow so bright you had to squint.

Grandad took the long tongs down from the wall and lifted the two glowing pieces out onto the anvil, and held them there, end to end, where they had broken.

Then he held out his hammer to Leo.

"Me?" said Leo.

"It's your sword, lad," said Grandad. "It chose you. So it's got to be your hands that mend it. I'll hold. You strike."

Leo took the hammer. It was so heavy he needed both hands.

"What do I do?"

"What you always do," said Grandad. "Look at it till you can see how it wants to go back together. Then be patient."

Leo looked at the two halves of the sword, blazing on the anvil. He looked at the crack between them.

He lifted the hammer and brought it down.

*Clang.*

"I'm scared," he said.

*Clang.*

"I'm going anyway."

*Clang.*

"I'm scared of the dark."

*Clang.*

"I'm scared of heights, and deep water, and the Pritchard twins, and Mrs Okafor asking me questions in class."

*Clang.*

"I'm scared I'm not good enough."

*Clang.*

"I'm going anyway. I'm going anyway. *I'm going anyway.*"

With every blow, the two pieces of the sword glowed brighter. Sparks flew up in fountains all around the anvil — but they weren't orange sparks. They were gold. And as Leo struck and struck, the crack between the two halves began to close. The steel flowed together. It joined.

"Now," said Grandad. "Quench it."

Leo took the tongs from him, and with the very last of his strength, he lifted the sword and plunged it into the great water trough beside the forge.

There was a hiss like a hundred angry cats, and a cloud of steam so thick it hid the whole hall.

Then the steam cleared.

And Leo lifted out the sword.

It was whole. Where it had broken, a hand's width above the hilt, there was a seam — a thin, bright, zigzag line of gold running right across the blade, like the lines on Grandma's mended bowl.

And it was shining.

Not like a star. Not a cold, faraway, blazing light that sent the darkness running away screaming. It shone like a hearth. Like a kitchen window at night when you're walking home cold and tired. Like coals glowing low in a forge while someone you love is working late. It was a warm, golden, steady light, and it filled the whole great hall of Cinder Keep, up to the ceiling and into every corner. And on the pommel, the lion's eyes were wide open, and they were gold as well.

Every Rustman in the hall stopped dead.

Then, one after another, they fell apart. Their empty armour came clattering to the floor in heaps of harmless scrap. The Scrapjaws' coals went out like blown candles. And the grey workers, standing in the golden light with their shovels and pokers raised, began — slowly, one by one — to blink, and look at each other, and turn back into colour.

"Wren?" said Tobias, in a voice full of wonder. "*Wren?*"

Wren flew into her father's arms.

* * *

But Leo wasn't looking at them.

He was looking at Edwin.

His great-uncle had fallen to his knees. The rust had covered almost all of him now — his face, his hair, his hands — and he was stiff and cracked and crumbling at the edges. Only his eyes were still his own, pale and wet and full of something that looked a lot like fear.

"Finish it, boy," he whispered. His voice was like dry leaves. "Go on. That's what swords are for."

Leo walked over to him with the shining sword in his hand. His legs were shaking. He knelt down on the cold stone floor.

"No," he said. "It isn't. Not this one."

And instead of raising the sword, he laid it down, flat and gentle, against his great-uncle's chest — right over his heart, where the rust was thickest.

"What is broken," said Leo, "I make whole."

Edwin gasped.

The golden light sank into him. It ran along the cracks in the rust like the gold in the mended bowl, filling every one of them — and then the rust began to fall away. It came off in flakes, and then in sheets, like old paint peeling off a wall, and underneath it was an old man. Just a thin, tired, ordinary old man, with Grandad's nose and Grandad's chin, and tears pouring down his face.

Grandad knelt down beside him. He took his brother's hand — a hand that wasn't rusty any more.

"I looked for you, Ed," said Grandad. "Every summer. For fifty years."

Edwin looked at him for a long time.

"I know," he said at last. "I know you did, Walt. I watched you. Every summer." His face crumpled. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I broke everything."

"Well," said Grandad, and he put his arms round his brother and held on tight. "It's a good thing we're Smiths, then. Isn't it?"

And through the tall windows of Cinder Keep, as the golden light poured out of them and across the waste, Leo saw the grey haze begin to thin, and tear, and blow away — and underneath it, very faintly, like the first breath of spring, the ground beginning to turn green.

## Chapter Twelve: The Keeper

It took them five days to walk home, because Leo insisted on stopping at the Nothing Gorge.

He mended the Old Bridge properly this time. It took the whole of one long afternoon. He sat on the edge of the gorge with his legs dangling over the mist (he found he could do that now, as long as he didn't think about it too much) and laid the sword against the stones and the chains. He didn't rush. He didn't hurry. He went slowly and carefully, one link at a time, one stone at a time, until every stone was sound and every chain was strong. When he'd finished, the stone lions at either end had lost all their rust and gleamed white in the sunset, and Leo was almost sure that one of them winked at him.

"There," he said. "That'll last."

Behind them, all across what had been the Greywaste, green was pushing up through the rust. Grass, and tiny flowers, and here and there, the first thin shoots of apple trees.

The Whispering Wood let them through without a word. Leo thought perhaps it had run out of things to say to him.

And when they came over the hill above Kettleby, the bell in the little tower was ringing — ringing and ringing, bright bronze again — and every chimney in the village was smoking, and from every workshop came the sound of hammers: *clang, clang, clang*, so loud you could hear it for miles.

Old Mother Tansy was waiting on the bridge with her arms folded.

"Took your time," she said to Leo. Then she looked at the sword at his side, and at the golden seam across its blade, and her one good eye went bright and wet.

"Well," she said, much more quietly. "Well, well, well."

* * *

Edwin stayed in Kettleby.

"I broke a lot of things," he said, standing awkwardly on the bridge with the whole village staring at him. Without his armour, he looked very small, and very old. "In this village, and all over Everhallow. It's going to take me a long time to mend them all." He swallowed. "I'd like to start. If you'll let me."

There was a long, uncomfortable silence.

Then Tobias stepped forward. He looked at Edwin for a while. And then he held out his hand.

"I could use someone to work the bellows," he said.

Edwin stared at the hand as though he had never seen one before. Then he shook it.

* * *

Wren walked with them all the way to the stone arch on the hillside.

For a while they stood in front of it, not saying anything. Through the arch, Leo could see Grandad's forge — dim and ordinary and waiting. Biscuit had already gone through, and was sitting on the other side, pretending not to listen.

"You'll come back," said Wren. It wasn't a question.

"I'm the Keeper," said Leo. "I sort of have to."

"Good." She scuffed her too-big boot in the grass. "Next time, I'll teach you to use a slingshot. You're hopeless with a sword."

"I know," said Leo. "It's not really for fighting."

Wren laughed. Then she punched him on the arm, quite hard, and then she hugged him, even harder.

"Thanks for getting my dad back," she said into his shoulder.

"Thanks for saving me from the Scrapjaws," said Leo. "And for the oatcake."

"It was a very squashed oatcake."

"It was the best oatcake I've ever had."

She let go, and grinned the grin that changed her whole face, and Leo ducked through the arch into Grandad's forge.

* * *

It was just as they'd left it — except that there was no rust anywhere. The tools gleamed on their hooks. The anvil was black and clean. Outside, through the forge door, the roses round the cottage windows were roses again, red and pink and smelling of summer.

Grandad came through last. He closed the little door behind him, and for a while he just stood there with his hand flat against the old black wood.

Biscuit cleared his throat.

"Right," he said. "Well. That was all very nice. Now listen, lad, because this is important." He fixed Leo with a stern look from under his enormous eyebrows. "Don't tell your mother I can talk."

"Why not?"

"Because she'll want me to explain where all the sausages went."

"What sausages?"

"*Exactly*," said Biscuit. "Woof."

And after that, on this side of the door, he never said another word. But sometimes, when Leo gave him his crusts, he would sigh very meaningfully.

* * *

That night, Grandad and Leo sat together in the forge with the fire banked low and the sword lying across Leo's knees.

"It's yours now, you know," said Grandad. "Properly yours. It chose you, and then you remade it with your own two hands. There's nobody else in any world it'll ever belong to."

Leo ran his finger along the golden seam in the blade.

"I gave it away," he said quietly. "To Edwin. I thought it might help him. And it got broken."

"It did," said Grandad. "And you mended it. And it came out better." He nodded across the yard, towards the cottage, where Grandma's bowl sat on the dresser with its lines of gold. "Some things do."

They were quiet for a while.

"A reforged sword needs a new name," said Grandad. "That's tradition."

"Everyone calls it the Magic Sword."

"That's not a name. That's a description. That's like calling Biscuit 'the Dog'."

Leo thought about it for a long time. He thought about bridges and bowls and gate hinges, and a grey man turning back into colour, and a rusted old king weeping on a stone floor.

"Mender," he said.

Grandad smiled, and his whole face folded up like a map.

"Mender," he said. "Aye. That'll do."

On the pommel, the lion opened one golden eye, and closed it again, and — Leo was quite sure this time — it smiled.

* * *

Leo spent the rest of the summer going back and forth through the little door.

He helped rebuild Kettleby. He learned to use a slingshot (badly). He learned to make a nail, and a hinge, and a horseshoe, and at last a small iron bird with wings that really folded, which he gave to Wren. He visited Edwin, who was quiet, and sad, and a little less sad every week, and who taught him to tell how hot iron was just by its colour — even better than Grandad could. And on one very special day, he watched the two brothers stand side by side and light the great forge in Cinder Keep together — not to make Rustmen, but to make ploughs, for the new farms in what used to be the Greywaste.

And then, at the end of August, Mum's car came bumping down the tunnel of hedges and into the yard.

She hugged Leo so hard his feet left the ground.

"Look at you!" she said, holding him at arm's length. "You've grown! You look — I don't know. Different." She peered at him. "Taller, somehow."

"I've been working in the forge," said Leo.

"Has he been all right, Dad? Did he have a nice, quiet summer?"

Leo and Grandad looked at each other.

"Mostly," said Leo.

* * *

Leo Smith is still afraid of quite a lot of things.

He is still a bit afraid of the dark. He is still afraid of deep water, and the Pritchard twins, and the moment in class when the teacher looks up from the register and says, "Let's hear from... Leo."

But he knows something now that he didn't know before.

He knows that being afraid and being brave are not opposites. That courage isn't something you feel — it's something you do, with your knees shaking, one step at a time. He knows that any fool can make a sword that breaks things, but it takes a Smith to make one that mends.

And he knows that anything broken can be mended, if you are patient with it.

Even bridges. Even swords.

Even people.

*Especially* people.

And somewhere, at the back of a forge in Hollowford, behind a little black door with a keyhole shaped like a lion's mane, a whole world is waiting for its Keeper to come back.

He always does.

**THE END**
